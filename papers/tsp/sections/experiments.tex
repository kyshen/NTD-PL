\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Questions and Protocol}

The experiments are organized around three questions. First, can the linked
model recover tensors whose observations contain a nonlinear response?
Second, under the same Tucker rank, does this response improve real
hyperspectral reconstruction and completion? Third, when the model helps, do
the gains follow the mechanism predicted by the analysis, and can this regime
be detected before fitting NTD-PL?

Tucker is the primary baseline because a comparison at the same Tucker rank
directly isolates the effect of the learned scalar response. We also compare
with CP and tensor train (TT) using similar parameter counts. These methods
provide broader low-rank tensor baselines and show whether the
improvement extends beyond one Tucker implementation.

For reconstruction, RMSE and SAM are computed on all tensor entries. For
completion, RMSE* and SAM* are computed only on entries hidden during fitting.
Hyperspectral cubes are globally normalized, and lower RMSE and SAM are better.
The primary real dataset is the CAVE multispectral image database
\cite{cave_multispectral_database,yasuma2010gapc}. All CAVE experiments use
all 15 scenes, each of size \(512\times512\times31\);
Fig.~\ref{fig:cave-scenes} shows their RGB previews. We also
include supplementary checks on external hyperspectral cubes and on
non-hyperspectral tensor data, including natural images, object-view tensors,
and action videos, to test whether the same response-based pattern appears
beyond the CAVE acquisition setting. Additional robustness, dataset, and
protocol details are provided in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Controlled Recovery Under Nonlinear Measurements}
\label{sec:exp-controlled}

The controlled experiment tests the basic mechanism in a setting where the
ground-truth Tucker structure is known. We start from an underlying Tucker
signal \(\cS^\star\) of known rank and observe every tensor entry. For nonlinear
responses, we add a response component that is
orthogonal to the underlying Tucker signal. Let \(\bar{\cS}\) denote the
normalized underlying signal and let \(\bar{\cR}\) denote the normalized
nonlinear residual after this projection. The noisy measurement is generated
as
\begin{equation}
  \cY_\alpha =
  \sqrt{1-\alpha}\,\bar{\cS}
  +
  \sqrt{\alpha}\,\bar{\cR}
  + \cE,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha\) controls the energy assigned to the nonlinear residual, and
\(\cE\) is additive Gaussian noise. The identity panel directly uses
\(\bar{\cS}\) for every \(\alpha\), so it is a repeated noisy linear control. This
construction keeps the intended Tucker rank fixed while making the measured
tensor increasingly mismatched to a linear Tucker observation model.

Figure~\ref{fig:controlled-alpha} shows the expected transition. We use 10
random seeds, Tucker rank \((4,4,4)\), and additive Gaussian noise at 30 dB SNR
for all panels. In the identity-control panel, Tucker and NTD-PL are nearly
identical, with mean RMSE about \(0.029\) for both methods across the sweep.
Thus the linked response does not create a spurious advantage when the
measurement is already linear.

The behavior changes once a nonlinear residual is present. As \(\alpha\)
increases, matched-rank Tucker must fit a measured tensor whose apparent rank
has grown, while NTD-PL can reuse the same Tucker signal through a learned
scalar response. The gap is therefore small in the nearly linear regime and
widens for stronger nonlinear measurements; for example, in the
\(\tanh(\kappa s)\) panel at \(\alpha=0.4\), Tucker reaches RMSE \(0.338\)
whereas NTD-PL remains near \(0.124\). This pattern is the desired mechanism
check: the advantage appears when the observation departs from a linear Tucker
measurement map, not because the model has extra coefficients.
Degree-dependent controls and optimization traces are reported in the
supplementary material.

\subsection{Real Hyperspectral Recovery on CAVE}
\label{sec:exp-cave}

We next test whether NTD-PL improves real
spectral-spatial tensors.

Reconstruction first tests whether the learned response improves accuracy at a
fixed Tucker rank. We compare NTD-PL with Tucker, CP, and TT at similar
parameter counts.
Table~\ref{tab:cave-main-baselines} compares the methods at approximately 13k,
27k, and 42k parameters. The CP and TT ranks are adjusted at each budget, and
NTD-PL is reported for \(P=2,4,6\). Increasing \(P\) consistently lowers both
RMSE and NMSE at every budget, with a larger improvement from \(P=2\) to
\(P=4\) and a smaller refinement from \(P=4\) to \(P=6\). Thus the same
degree-saturation pattern holds across model capacities rather than only at one
selected rank.

NTD-PL with \(P=6\) gives the lowest mean RMSE at all three budgets. Relative
to the strongest alternative at each budget, its RMSE reduction is 11.1\% at
13k and 6.1\% at 27k; at 42k it is essentially tied with CP and remains below
Tucker. The NMSE results have the same ordering at the low and medium budgets,
whereas CP has the best high-budget NMSE. RMSE and NMSE are averaged
scene-wise, but NMSE first normalizes each scene by its own energy, so their
order need not coincide. The narrowing gap is expected: as the base tensor rank
grows, ordinary low-rank models can absorb more of the measured structure and
the marginal value of the response decreases.

Across the tested Tucker ranks, NTD-PL also consistently improves matched-rank
Tucker in both RMSE and SAM, with larger gains at lower ranks and smaller gains
as the Tucker rank grows. This is the expected behavior of a fixed-rank
nonlinear correction: if ordinary multilinear rank is already high enough to
absorb the nonlinear response, the marginal value of the response should
decrease.

Table~\ref{tab:rank-lift-tucker} asks a complementary capacity question: how
much ordinary Tucker rank is needed to match a lower-rank NTD-PL model? Across
the tested ranks, Tucker needs roughly 51--60\% more parameters to match the
NTD-PL RMSE, while NTD-PL still has lower SAM. The result supports the
rank-inflation interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:theory}: the power response
reuses the same low-rank Tucker signal to express structured nonlinear
residuals with a small number of response coefficients.

Completion then tests whether this fixed-rank benefit transfers to entries not
used for fitting. Table~\ref{tab:cave-completion-main} reports CAVE
completion under random masks using all 15 scenes. At the same Tucker rank,
NTD-PL improves RMSE* by
12.5--17.7\% and SAM* by 13.4--29.3\% across the tested missing
rates. Additional observation-noise results are reported in
the supplementary material.

\subsection{Mechanism: Rank Inflation and Learned Response}
\label{sec:exp-mechanism}

The Tucker polynomial view says that higher degrees add interactions among the
same core coordinates, while the shared-factor form expresses those terms as
powers of one Tucker tensor. We examine this mechanism by varying the maximum
degree \(P\) on the CAVE scenes while keeping the Tucker rank at
\((12,12,4)\). This lets us track the rank added by the higher-order terms and
the learned scalar map \(s\mapsto f_{\bbeta}(s)\).

For each scene and each \(P\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}\), we fit a matched-rank Tucker
model and an NTD-PL model using all entries of the normalized cube. Let \(\widehat{\cS}\)
be the Tucker signal reconstructed from the NTD-PL core and factors, and let
\(\widehat{\cY}\) be its fitted prediction after the scalar response. We isolate
the nonlinear response component
\(\widehat{\cR}_{\mathrm{nl}}=\widehat{\cY}-\widehat\beta_0\mathbf{1}
-\widehat\beta_1\widehat{\cS}
\) and compute singular-value spectra of its mode-1 and
mode-2 unfoldings. The rank reported in
Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism-rank-inflation} is the number of singular values needed
to capture 99.9\% of squared singular-value energy; it is therefore an
energy-based effective rank, rather than the exact algebraic rank. The bars
show the mean RMSE reduction of NTD-PL relative to matched-rank Tucker across
the 15 scenes, while the annotations show the median nonlinear-response rank
and the median effective-rank increase over \(\widehat{\cS}\), respectively.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/mechanism_rank_inflation.pdf}
  \caption{Rank-inflation evidence on CAVE reconstruction at Tucker
  rank \((12,12,4)\). Bars show the mean RMSE reduction relative to
  matched-rank Tucker. Labels report \(r_{\mathrm{nl}}\), the median
  99.9\%-energy rank of the nonlinear response component, and, when positive,
  the median effective-rank increase over the NTD-PL Tucker signal.}
  \label{fig:mechanism-rank-inflation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism-rank-inflation} shows the rank-inflation trend. At
\(P=2\), the nonlinear component already has nonzero effective rank, but its
energy is not yet sufficient to increase the median rank of the final
prediction. A median rank increase of \(2.0\) appears at \(P=3\), then
increases gradually to \(5.0\) at \(P=6\), while the mean RMSE gain rises from
2.82\% to 8.79\%. The largest change occurs between \(P=2\) and \(P=4\);
higher degrees provide smaller refinements, while the learned Tucker signal
remains at the prescribed rank. Across scenes and degrees, spatial tail capture
by the NTD-PL prediction has Spearman correlation \(0.86\) with RMSE gain,
indicating that the improvement is tied to how much high-rank residual
structure the response accounts for.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism-response} shows the corresponding learned response
curves. For each scene and each \(P\), we evaluate the fitted polynomial at 101
empirical quantiles between the 1st and 99th percentiles of the learned Tucker
signal. Each curve is then normalized by its endpoint values, so both axes run
from 0 to 1 and the dashed diagonal is the identity response. Thin curves show
individual scenes and the dark curve is their pointwise mean. The title gain is
the mean RMSE reduction relative to the \(P=1\) model. The \(P=1\) model is
linear by construction. Allowing \(P=2\) and
\(P=4\) bends the shared response away from the identity map and yields most of
the gain; \(P=6\) mainly refines the same shape. Detailed mechanism tables are
given in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Operating Regimes and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:exp-diagnostics}

The previous experiments explain why NTD-PL helps when the Tucker residual is
response-like. We finally ask whether this condition can be detected before
joint NTD-PL training, and where the gain appears when the model is useful.

On CAVE reconstruction, the error reduction is not uniform over the cube. When
entries are grouped by normalized observed value and spectral regime, the
largest gains occur in middle intensity deciles and the 510--700 nm regimes,
while a few low-yield bins remain near zero. This supports the same
operating-regime view: the response is useful when the Tucker residual has
coherent structure in the observed domain rather than unstructured noise.
Figure~\ref{fig:error-regime} visualizes the marginal and joint regimes; the
supplementary material reports the corresponding numerical table.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/nonhsi_dlink_scatter_3domain.pdf}
  \caption{Cross-domain response diagnostic. Each point plots the diagnostic
  \(\Dlink\) (dB) against the RMSE gain of NTD-PL over Tucker. Larger
  diagnostic scores predict larger benefits from the learned response.}
  \label{fig:nonhsi-link-scatter}
\end{figure*}

The diagnostic \(\Dlink\) is computed by fitting a Tucker model and measuring,
in dB, the residual-energy reduction obtained by refitting only the response coefficients. It
is therefore substantially cheaper than joint NTD-PL training and is used here
as a model-selection signal.

On CAVE reconstruction, treating each scene as one tensor unit gives a similar
trend: high-\(\Dlink\) scenes have larger eventual
NTD-PL gains than low-\(\Dlink\) scenes. The CAVE row is reported together
with the cross-domain diagnostic statistics in the supplementary material.

Figure~\ref{fig:nonhsi-link-scatter} evaluates the same diagnostic beyond
hyperspectral data. Each point is an independently fitted tensor unit, and the
horizontal axis is \(\Dlink\) measured before joint NTD-PL training. Object-view
tensors show the clearest separation, action-video tensors follow the same
trend, and natural images occupy a lower-yield but still positive regime. These
results close the loop: NTD-PL is most effective when a matched-rank Tucker fit
leaves residual structure that can be reduced by a shared entrywise response.
